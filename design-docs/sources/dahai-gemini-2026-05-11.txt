Imagine a "water world" in a sci-fi setting. Perhaps it has a hundred-kilometer deep ocean, with no "terran" land (it might have "land" in the form of icecaps, but these would be different). Can you characterize what such a world might be like?

A world with a 100-kilometer deep ocean is a fascinating exercise in extreme physics. On Earth, the ocean averages about 3.7 km deep, and the deepest point (the Mariana Trench) is only 11 km. At 100 km, we aren't just talking about "more water"—we are talking about water behaving in ways that feel like science fiction.

Here is a breakdown of the physical and structural characteristics of such a "Hycean" or water-giant world.

1. The Vertical Layers: Not Just Wet

In a 100-km deep ocean, the sheer weight of the water column creates pressures that fundamentally change the state of matter.

The Sunlight Zone (Epipelagic): The top 200 meters. This is the only place where photosynthesis can happen. In a world with no continents to weather and provide minerals, this layer might be nutrient-poor unless there is significant volcanic activity or "marine snow" recycling.

The Twilight & Midnight Zones: Extending down several kilometers. It would be pitch black, silent, and increasingly pressurized.

The Hadal Abyss: On Earth, this is the bottom. On your world, this is barely the 10% mark.

The Exotic Ice Floor: This is the most "sci-fi" part. At a depth of roughly 60–100 km (depending on the planet's gravity and temperature), the pressure becomes so intense—exceeding 2–3 Gigapascals—that water is forced into a solid state even if it is warm. This creates a seafloor made of Ice VII or Ice VI.

2. A World Without "Dirt"

On Earth, the ocean is "fertilized" by dust blowing off deserts and minerals washing off mountains via rivers. On a total water world:

Nutrient Starvation: Without land, the upper ocean might be a biological desert. Life would rely almost entirely on hydrothermal vents.

The Mineral Gap: On Earth, the water touches silicate rock (the crust). On your world, the liquid water is separated from the rocky core by a massive shell of high-pressure ice. This "Ice Trap" prevents essential minerals from rising into the liquid ocean, potentially making the water incredibly pure—and incredibly hostile to life as we know it.

3. The Atmosphere and Weather

Without landmasses to break up wind patterns, the weather would be terrifyingly efficient.

Super-Cyclones: Hurricanes would have no "landfall" to weaken them. They could grow to planetary scales and persist for years, similar to the Great Red Spot on Jupiter.

Jet Streams: The planet would likely have very distinct, high-speed latitudinal wind bands.

Humidity: The air would be permanently saturated. "Rain" might feel less like a drizzle and more like the sky simply collapsing.

4. Exotic "Land" (Icecaps and More)

Since you mentioned no terran land, the only solid surfaces would be:

Glacial Platforms: Massive, floating ice sheets at the poles. Because the ocean is so deep, these wouldn't be "grounded" on rock; they would be gargantuan icebergs the size of continents, drifting slowly according to deep-water currents.

Pumice Rafts or Bio-Mats: In a sci-fi twist, "land" could be biological—massive, tangled webs of floating flora or calcified remains of ancient sea creatures forming floating islands.

5. Potential for Life

If life exists, it would have to solve the "Mineral Problem."

Vertical Migrators: Organisms might evolve to dive deep to the ice-floor to "mine" trapped minerals and bring them back to the sunlit surface.

Chemosynthesis: Most life would likely cluster around "hotspots" where the high-pressure ice is thin enough for volcanic heat from the core to create plumes of mineral-rich water.

The Physics Reality Check: Because water is heavy, a planet with a 100-km ocean would likely have significantly higher surface gravity than Earth if it has a similar rocky core. Walking on one of those icecaps might feel like you're wearing a heavy lead vest.

You mentioned desalination. Why would the water be salty?

That is a great catch. On Earth, we often think of salt as a "land" product—mineral runoff from rocks carried by rivers into the sea. You might assume that without exposed continents to erode, the ocean would stay fresh.

However, in planetary science, "salty" is the default state for liquid water on a massive scale for a few key reasons:

1. The Primordial Delivery

Planets are formed from a disk of gas and dust. Much of the water on a world like this would be delivered by carbonaceous chondrites (asteroids) and comets. These space rocks aren't just frozen pure water; they are "dirty" snowballs packed with soluble salts, sulfur, and magnesium. From the very moment the planet coalesced and the ice melted into an ocean, the water would have been a brine.

2. Interaction with the Core

Even if the water started pure, it wouldn't stay that way. Underneath that 100-km ocean and the layer of exotic ice lies the silicate core (the rocky center).

Volcanic Leaching: The core’s internal heat would drive hydrothermal activity. Superheated water would circulate through the rocky crust, dissolving minerals like sodium, chlorine, calcium, and potassium.

Diffusion: Over billions of years, these minerals migrate upward through the high-pressure ice layers via cracks or molecular diffusion, eventually saturating the liquid ocean.

3. High-Pressure Ice as a "Filter"

Interestingly, the 100-km depth actually creates a concentration effect. When water freezes into those exotic high-pressure ices (like Ice VI or VII) at the bottom, the crystal structure of the ice often "rejects" salt molecules—a process called brine rejection.

As the bottom of the ocean freezes into ice due to pressure, the salts are pushed back up into the liquid layer. Over eons, this makes the liquid ocean saltier and saltier while the "ice floor" remains relatively pure.

Why this is a problem for colonists

For a human colonist, "salt" isn't just one thing. It's a mix of ions that makes the water undrinkable and, more importantly, highly corrosive.

Engineering Stress: A 100-km deep ocean of brine is a massive battery. The salinity creates an electrolyte-rich environment that would eat through standard steel hulls in months via galvanic corrosion.

Energy Intensive: To get drinking water, colonists can't just boil it (which takes massive energy). They would likely use Reverse Osmosis, forcing the seawater through membranes at high pressure.

When you have a 100-km deep ocean, the "ice floor" isn't a smooth, solid sheet like a skating rink. It’s a chaotic, high-pressure transition zone.

1. The "Snowing Upwards" Phenomenon

In Ganymede's interior, the pressure-temperature balance creates a bizarre inversion of how we think about freezing.

At these extreme depths, as the water begins to turn into Ice VI, the crystals are actually denser than the surrounding liquid, so they "sink" toward the core.

However, as they sink, they can trap pockets of salty water (brine) within their crystal lattice.

Because salt lowers the freezing point of water, these pockets remain liquid even as the pressure mounts. This creates a "mushy" mantle—a slush of high-pressure ice and hyper-concentrated brine.

2. Why Salt Doesn't Get "Rejected" Upward

On a surface glacier, salt is pushed out because there is room for the brine to flow away. But at the bottom of a 100-km ocean:

The Trap: The ice is forming under such immense weight that the brine has nowhere to go. It gets squeezed into the gaps between ice crystals.

Convection: These trapped brine pockets are much saltier (and therefore denser) than the ocean above. Instead of rising, they tend to stay "heavy" and locked in the ice.

The Sandwich: This creates a "Club Sandwich" model: a liquid ocean on top, a layer of mushy ice-and-brine in the middle, and then perhaps another layer of liquid water below that, touching the rocky core.

3. Implications for Your Colonists

If Ganymede is the template, the "Seafloor" isn't a solid place to land a probe or a mine.

The "Quicksand" Floor: A colony attempting to land on the Ice VI floor wouldn't find a solid surface. They would find a shifting, high-pressure "slush" that might slowly swallow any heavy machinery.

Brine Mining: This is actually a massive win for colonists. Instead of having to drill through kilometers of rock to get minerals, they could simply pump out the trapped brine pockets. These pockets would be "distilled" mineral soups—concentrated reservoirs of the planet's entire geological history.

The Heat Paradox: This mushy layer acts as an insulator. It keeps the heat from the rocky core trapped, which might maintain the liquid ocean for billions of years longer than a solid ice sheet would.

For surface water to be salty, minerals from the rocky core have to travel up through 100 kilometers of water and ice.

The Stratified Model (Low Salinity): If the ocean is very calm, the heavy salts might stay at the bottom. The surface would be mostly "fresh" water (melted ice/comet water). In this scenario, surface colonists would struggle to find minerals for life or industry.

The Convective Model (High Salinity): If the core is hot, it creates convection. Hot, mineral-rich water at the bottom becomes less dense and rises, while cold water sinks. This turns the entire 100-km ocean into a "conveyor belt," bringing salt to the surface.

2. The Ganymede "Club Sandwich"

If we follow the Ganymede model, you have a layer of high-pressure ice (Ice VI) sitting between the rocky core and the upper ocean. You might think this acts as a barrier, but it’s actually more like a leaky sponge.

The "Salt Pump": Because salt lowers the freezing point of water, these brines stay liquid even when the surrounding water freezes into Ice VI. These brines are incredibly dense and mineral-heavy. The Result: A world with Earth's gravity would have a "thinner" liquid layer before hitting the ice. This makes it easier for volcanic heat and minerals to reach the surface because the "conveyor belt" has a shorter distance to travel.

The following concepts are grounded in current planetary science research regarding the salinity and stratification of deep-ocean worlds:

1. The Salinity of Surface Oceans

Research into exoplanets and icy moons suggests that even with a massive water column, surface salinity is maintained through convection. If the rocky core is geologically active, the heat prevents the ocean from becoming a stagnant, layered pond.

Mineral Transport: Models of "Ocean Worlds" indicate that hydrothermal activity at the silicate-water interface releases ions (salts) that are transported to the upper layers via large-scale convective plumes. Without this, life-essential elements like phosphorus would be trapped at the bottom.

Initial Salinity: It is widely theorized that the water delivered to these planets via carbonaceous chondrites was already rich in volatiles and salts, meaning the ocean likely started saline rather than fresh.

2. The "Mushy" Layer and Brine Trapping (The Ganymede Model)

The concept of the "mushy" high-pressure ice layer comes largely from the work of Steve Vance and colleagues, who modeled the interior of Ganymede.

The Layered Structure: Vance et al. (2014) proposed that Ganymede’s ocean might be a "club sandwich" of up to three layers of liquid ocean separated by different phases of high-pressure ice (Ice VI and Ice V).

Brine Inclusion: Their research suggests that these high-pressure ice layers are not pure. Instead, they contain trapped pockets of concentrated brine. Because salt lowers the freezing point, these pockets stay liquid even as the surrounding water crystallizes into exotic ice.

Upward Transport: Subsequent studies have explored "salt fountaining," where dense brines can be forced upward through cracks or thermal conduits in the ice, potentially enriching the upper ocean with salts from the deep interior.

3. Stability and Stratification

A key debate in the literature is whether these oceans are stratified (like a layer cake) or well-mixed.

Stratification: If the ocean is too deep, the density difference between the cold, fresh surface and the warm, salty deep can prevent mixing. This would leave the surface "fresh" but biologically dead.

Mixing: However, on a planet with Earth-standard gravity and a hot core, the thermal gradient is usually strong enough to overcome this stratification, leading to a saline surface ocean similar to Earth’s, though with a different chemical "fingerprint" (higher in magnesium and sulfates).

On a world with no landmasses to provide friction or "break" the wind, the Coriolis effect becomes the undisputed master of the planet’s climate. This would result in a atmospheric structure similar to Jupiter or Saturn, but with a water-vapor-driven "engine" rather than hydrogen/helium.

This doesn't just make the world more dramatic—it actually solves your Phosphorus Paradox through sheer mechanical force.

1. The Development of Permanent Storm Bands

On Earth, continents disrupt air flow. On a water world, the wind can accelerate indefinitely until it hits a balance point of internal friction.

Zonal Flow: You would see distinct "bands" of wind moving in opposite directions at different latitudes. At the boundaries of these bands, where "east-bound" air rubs against "west-bound" air, you get massive, permanent shear zones.

The Hurricane Belt: These shear zones would be the breeding grounds for "infinite" hurricanes. Without land to sap their energy, a storm could orbit the planet multiple times, potentially lasting for years.

Super-Saturation: The atmosphere would be incredibly thick with water vapor, creating a high-pressure environment at the surface, which might actually help humans mitigate some "bends" related issues if they dive shallowly.

2. How Hurricanes Solve the Nutrient Problem

The biggest problem for your aquaculture was the "Bio-Pump"—nutrients sinking into the abyss. Strong hurricanes provide the perfect solution: Ekman Pumping and Deep Mixing.

The Upwelling Engine: A hurricane is essentially a massive vacuum cleaner. The low pressure at the center of a storm, combined with the Coriolis-driven "Ekman suction," pulls water from the depths toward the surface.
