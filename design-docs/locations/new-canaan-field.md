# New Canaan Field

New Canaan Field was not named ironically at first. During the first Belt claim rush, the little co-orbital cluster beside the Caravanserai proved rich in titanium-bearing stone: ilmenite-heavy veins, rutile pockets, nickel-titanium feedstock, and clean oxide-metal concentrates that could be crushed, separated, and shipped without a long interplanetary haul. The early miners did not need to send raw rock down a gravity well. They built concentrator galleries, sponge furnaces, assay offices, billet presses, and cargo yards into the asteroids themselves, exporting high-grade concentrate and semi-refined stock to the young Belt, the Camps, and the first serious drydock operations. For a while the promised land was real.

The boom built towns as well as mines. Harlan's Station, Mercer's Claim, and a scatter of smaller diggings once supported warm halls, machine shops, claim courts, chapels, gambling rooms, family habs, and bright civic spaces named with the old optimism: Promise Exchange, First Landing, Temple of Plenty. The New Canaan Miners Mutual formed in those days as a cooperative of independent claimholders, pooling money for shared refineries, emergency stores, tugs, lawyers, and relief funds. Its charter was part business compact and part frontier ethic: no claim jumped under distress, no neighbor left cold, no lawyer called before the board had heard both sides.

Then Gaia's money arrived. The old families still call them lawyers and pen-pushers with big money: corporate survey teams, finance-city brokers, and polished holding-company agents who bought distressed claims in bundles, challenged handshake boundaries, and strip-mined the crown jewels with equipment no family crew could match. The Miners Mutual fought some cases, settled others, and saved what it could, but the best rocks were hollowed, fractured, or folded into companies that lost interest as soon as margins fell. New strikes farther out and heavier industry in the Camps made New Canaan's remaining ore look small, dirty, and expensive.

Today the Field still works, but on dull margins. The Co-op buys tailings concentrate, basalt fiber feedstock, bulk silicates, regolith shielding blocks, scrap pressure alloy, and stubborn little assay lots from its members, then tries to find buyers with better processors: Camps foundries, drydocks, component shops, and occasional Belt construction yards. It does not send much to the Caravanserai except emergency salvage, paperwork cargo, or small brokered lots; Serai prices are too high and the hub's great players have little use for New Canaan's leftovers. The Co-op's real service to its members is the other direction: sourcing filters, recycler membranes, pressure seals, pump cartridges, rotary bearing kits, oxygen bottles, water drums, and life-support consumables in bulk from cheaper suppliers before distributing them through Harlan's Station and Mercer's Claim.

The physical field shows the same downward slide. Spin is cheap; warmth is dear. Old drums still turn around cold cores because stopping them is not worth the trouble, but only a few corridors, shops, and dock offices remain pressurized. A station built to house ten halls may keep one chapel, one machine room, and one board office alive while the rest hangs dark behind sealed pressure doors. The Miners Mutual's board is still dominated by descendants of the original settlers, including the Harlans and Mercers, who keep the old forms because the forms are what they have left. They extend oxygen credit after seal failures, arbitrate disputes by memory as much as law, and take in ruined gamblers, blacklisted dockhands, stranded Highliner washouts, and debtors from the Caravanserai with visible sadness. In their telling, the founders came to New Canaan to build something; the newcomers arrive because they have nowhere else to fall. The judgment is not always fair, but it is the sound of a promised land growing old in the shadow of a hub that barely notices it.
